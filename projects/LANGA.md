# LANGA — Language Gaming

## Project Rationale

Learning a new language as an adult is hard, and most of what we know about it comes from testing people *after* the fact — a vocabulary test at the end of a study session tells us what was learned, but not how the learning happened. Brain activity gives us a way to watch the process as it unfolds. Electroencephalography (EEG) measures voltages generated by the brain with millisecond precision, which means we can track a learner's response to a new word or an unfamiliar grammatical structure from the very first time they encounter it, trial by trial, rather than only at the end.

Language-learning apps are now how a great many adults actually study a new language. That makes them a realistic setting in which to study acquisition — and, eventually, a place to put the findings to work.

## Project Description

The LANGA (LANguage GAming) project studies changes in the brain as people first learn a new language. Our lab has developed games modelled on popular language-learning apps, and we record EEG while participants play them. Because the games are ours, we control exactly what each learner sees and when, and we can time-lock the EEG recording precisely to each item — so the brain responses can be tied to specific vocabulary items and specific grammatical rules as they are being acquired.

Over the course of play, we track how those brain responses change: how the response to a newly learned word shifts as it becomes familiar, and how sensitivity to the grammatical regularities of the new language emerges.

## Where This Is Headed

The longer-term goal is a brain-computer interface for language learning: an app that reads the learner's brain activity and adapts on the fly — revisiting material the brain signals suggest has not yet been consolidated, and moving on when it has. This connects LANGA to the lab's [brain-computer interface work](BCI).
